## 1. Problem Understanding

A bank must stop fraudulent transactions without disrupting honest customers. Fraud is **1.77%** of our 20,000 training transactions (353 cases) but **about 4% of the dollars** (average fraud $571 vs $247), so accuracy is the wrong yardstick: a model that never flags anything is 98.2% accurate and saves nothing. We therefore optimise **PR-AUC** (how well frauds are ranked above legitimate transactions) and report **precision, recall and fraud dollars caught at an operating threshold**, because every false alarm is a blocked customer. Singapore context sharpens this: MAS's Shared Responsibility Framework requires a 12-hour cooling-off after a login from a new device, and MAS itself notes that "a substantial number of flagged transactions are ultimately legitimate". Our model's output is a calibrated probability, so the bank can choose the trade-off explicitly.

## 2. Data Processing & Feature Engineering

The data is clean (no duplicates, impossible values or unseen categories), but it hides a trap: rows with a blank `merchant_category` or `new_device` are **never** fraud in training (0 of 113 and 0 of 124), an artefact of how the data was generated, and the test set has about four times more blanks. We impute every blank with a fixed training median or mode so the model cannot learn "missing means safe". The test set is also shifted: larger amounts, more new devices, and 9% of rows above $2,000 (vs 2.3% in training), mostly from old accounts that are rarely fraudulent. From the 10 raw columns we build 29 features: log scales for amount, age and spend; the amount relative to the day's spend and to account age; velocity bursts; threshold flags where the fraud rate jumps (amount > $500, account < 180 days, 3+ transactions in an hour); and interactions the data shows are multiplicative (new device on a young account is 17% fraud; new device with 5+ transactions in an hour is 38%). A `big_old` flag explicitly marks large purchases on long-standing accounts as low risk, so the model is not fooled by the test set's loyal big spenders.

## 3. Model Selection & Justification

We compared about 65 candidates on identical repeated folds: logistic regression, LightGBM, XGBoost, sklearn boosting, random forests, neural networks, SVMs, stacking, blends, class weighting, interaction searches and pseudo-labelling. The shipped model is **logistic regression on the 29 engineered features**. It assumes additive log-odds, which holds once the interactions are supplied explicitly, and it scored **0.234 ± 0.037 PR-AUC** against **0.195 ± 0.045** for an untuned LightGBM baseline and 0.018 for guessing, a 13× lift. A 50/50 blend with a shallow LightGBM tied in cross-validation and scored lower on the public leaderboard (0.169 vs 0.179), so we kept the simpler model: it needs only scikit-learn at inference, gives exact per-transaction reason codes (coefficient × value), and its probabilities are calibrated by construction. We rejected class weights and resampling because they inflate every probability (Brier 0.023 vs 0.015), which matters when the output drives a cost-based decision.

## 4. Evaluation Strategy

With 353 positives a single split swings by ±0.03 PR-AUC, more than the gap between good models, so every number is a mean over **repeated stratified cross-validation** (3×5 in the notebook, 5×5 for model selection), and models were only switched when a paired fold-by-fold test agreed. We scored three views: plain, with extra blanks injected into validation rows at the test set's rates (0.231 vs 0.231, no loss), and on the 20% of training rows most similar to the test set. Calibration was checked with reliability curves (predicted and actual fraud rates match in every decile; isotonic recalibration made it worse) and the Brier score (0.0151 vs 0.0173 for the base rate). For operations we price each alert: flag when probability × amount exceeds the review cost, which catches 87% of fraud dollars while flagging 14% of transactions. We also checked fairness under MAS's FEAT principles: at a 10% threshold, legitimate Indonesian and Australian customers are flagged about six times more often than Singaporeans; dropping `country` costs only 0.002 PR-AUC, so a fairness-constrained variant is cheap.

## 5. Limitations & Risks

About 1 in 6 frauds shows no observable signal (known device, old account, normal amount, no burst) and is caught by no model; most are Singapore-based, which is why recall on domestic fraud is low. There is no customer ID or timestamp, so per-customer behaviour baselines, the strongest real-world fraud features, are impossible here. The private test set differs from training, so public ranks within ±0.03 are noise and we did not tune to the leaderboard. With production data we would add customer history, monitor calibration drift ("does 30% still mean 30%?"), and retrain on a schedule.
